Introduction to Pronouns

a word that stands in for a nounA&G §§20.c, 142–152, 295–315

A pronoun stands in for a noun so that you don't have to repeat it. In "Marcus came home, and I saw him," him stands in for Marcus. A pronoun can also point to something (this, that), ask about it (who?), or refer to someone without saying who (someone). A&G puts it this way: a pronoun picks out a person or thing "without either naming or describing it" (§20.c).

Latin pronouns decline, like nouns: they have case, number and, most of them, gender. A pronoun takes its gender and number from the noun it stands for, which is called its antecedent, and its case from its own function in its own sentence.

The possessives, meus, tuus, suus, noster, vester (my, your, his own, our, your), come from the personal and reflexive pronouns but decline like the adjective bonus, -a, -um (A&G §145).

Pronoun or adjective?

Most pronouns can be used in two ways. On their own, they replace a noun: hic, this man, he; aliquis, someone. With a noun, they work as adjectives and agree with it in case, number and gender: hic vir, this man; aliquī philosophī, some philosophers. A few pronouns have slightly different forms for the two uses, mostly in the neuter: quid? (what?) on its own, but quod bellum? (which war?) with a noun.

How pronouns decline A&G §§142–152

Pronouns use the same five cases as nouns, and many of their endings are noun endings: -m in the accusative singular (illum, quem), -ōrum and -ārum in the genitive plural, -a in the neuter plural. A few endings belong only to pronouns, and these are the ones to learn first.

-īus and -ī

Genitive and dative singular

Most pronouns have -īus in the genitive singular and -ī in the dative singular, and they use the same form for all three genders. Is, hic and quī do the same with their own spellings.

illīus, illī: of that, to thateius, eī; huius, huic; cuius, cui

-d

Neuter nominative and accusative singular

Where a noun or adjective would have -um, many pronouns end the neuter singular in -d.

id, illud, istud, quod, quid, aliquidbut ipsum, and hoc

No gender

The 1st and 2nd person, and the reflexive

Ego, tū, nōs, vōs and suī have one set of forms for everyone, since the speaker and listener can be anyone. (Is, ea, id, the 3rd person, does have gender.) Suī also has no nominative, because it always points back to the subject.

mē: me (a man or a woman)sē: himself, herself, itself, themselves

What makes pronouns hard

He, she, it: is, ea, id

For he, she and it, Latin uses is, ea, id, which is both the 3rd-person personal pronoun and a demonstrative, and very often no pronoun at all, because the verb's ending already says who is acting: venit, he comes. When you translate is, ea, id, choose he, she, it or they by what the antecedent is in English, not by the Latin gender.

His own or his? suus and eius

English "his" can mean the subject's own or someone else's. Latin keeps them apart. Suus means the subject's own (Mārcus suum equum videt, Marcus sees his own horse); eius means someone else's (Mārcus eius equum videt, Marcus sees his, another man's, horse). The reflexive page explains the rule.
