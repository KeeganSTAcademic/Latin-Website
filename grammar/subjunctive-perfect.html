Perfect Subjunctive

Highlight a pattern
View
amāverit: may have lovedA&G §§450, 483

The perfect subjunctive is the complete subjunctive of primary sequence. In a dependent clause after a present or future main verb, it describes an action earlier than the main verb: rogō quid amāverit, "I ask what he loved." In a main clause it gives a prohibition with nē: nē timueris, "don't be afraid."

For what the subjunctive is and how sequence of tenses works, see The Subjunctive: Overview. Every use is listed on Uses of the Subjunctive.

The forms

Irregular: sum, esse (to be)

sum is regular here: the stem fu- plus -erim, fuerim, fuerīs, fuerit.

How to build it

Take the complete stem, the 3rd principal part minus -ī, and add -eri- with the personal endings: amāv-eri-t. These forms look like the future perfect, except in the 1st singular (amāverim, not amāverō). The passive is the perfect participle with the present subjunctive of sum: amātus sim, amātus sit.

The basic use: after a present or future main verb A&G §483

In a dependent clause after a present or future main verb, the perfect subjunctive shows an action earlier than the main verb. Translate it with an English past or "has ___ed": rogō quid fēcerit, "I ask what he did" or "what he has done."

How to spot it: Look for the complete stem with -eri- before the ending (amāverit, monuerit, rēxerit), or a participle with sim, sīs, sit.

Rogō quid fēcerit.
I ask what he did.
Nesciō cūr vēnerint.
I don't know why they came.

Other uses

Prohibitions

A&G §450

Latin forbids an action in three ways: nōlī with an infinitive (the usual way), cavē with the present subjunctive, or nē with the perfect subjunctive, which is blunter. In a prohibition the perfect does not refer to the past.

Clue: Nē with a 2nd person perfect subjunctive as the main verb.

Nē timueris.
Don't be afraid.

Other forms you'll see

Roman writers often shortened the perfect subjunctive, as they did the perfect indicative (A&G §181).
