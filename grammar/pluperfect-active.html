The pluperfect tense

The Latin pluperfect tense describes complete action that occurred in the past. We translate it like "he had loved." Its formation is incredibly simple: we attach the imperfect of the verb sum (eram, erās, erat …) to the complete stem, the 3rd principal part minus its -ī.

This is a way of marking time very similar to the -ba- seen in the imperfect, with the same -a- as a time vowel. We may understand the pluperfect as the combination of a stem communicating completed aspect, an -a- vowel marking past time, and personal endings marking the person and number of a verb.

Has loved or had loved?

It can be easy to mix up "he has loved" (completed present, the perfect tense) and "he had loved" (completed past, the pluperfect tense). The gradation in translation is subtle, but compare telling your boss when you are late for work that you "have arrived" versus that you "had arrived."

In the former case you have just got to work, perhaps just come through the door. The action of coming is over, but it happened now, in present time. If you told them that you "had arrived," you are implying your arrival happened sometime in the past. It implies that you did arrive, but that something happened between now and then. Maybe you had a family emergency and had to go home; maybe you left some critical materials in your car. Either way, your boss is going to think you aren't at work.
