Imperfect Subjunctive

The imperfect subjunctive is the incomplete subjunctive of secondary sequence. In a dependent clause after a past main verb, it describes an action at the same time as the main verb or later: rogāvī quid amāret, "I asked what he loved." On its own it expresses a wish or a condition that is not true now: utinam adesset, "if only he were here." It is the most common subjunctive in stories.

For what the subjunctive is and how sequence of tenses works, see The Subjunctive: Overview. Every use is listed on Uses of the Subjunctive.

The forms

Irregular: sum, esse (to be)

The rule works for irregular verbs too: esse gives essem, posse gives possem, ferre gives ferrem.

How to build it

Take the present infinitive, the 2nd principal part (amāre, monēre, regere, capere, audīre), and add the personal endings. The e of -re- is long before most endings (amārēs, amārētur) and short before -m, -t, -nt, final -r and -ntur.

The basic use: after a past main verb A&G §483

In a dependent clause after a past main verb, the imperfect subjunctive shows an action at the same time as the main verb or later. Translate it with an English past tense, not with "might": rogāvī quid faceret, "I asked what he was doing."

How to spot it: Look for the present infinitive with a personal ending on it: amāre-t, regere-t, audīre-t; for sum, esse-t.

Rogāvī quid facerēs.
I asked what you were doing.
Scrīpsit ut nōs monēret.
He wrote to warn us.
Tam fessus erat ut dormīret.
He was so tired that he fell asleep.

Other uses

"When" with cum

A&G §546

Cum with the indicative dates an action: it says when it happened. With the imperfect subjunctive it describes the situation at the time instead. English makes the same distinction without a change of form: "he had a fever when he was in Spain" dates the fever, but "Columbus found America when he was looking for India" describes his situation.

Clue: Cum followed by an imperfect subjunctive, with a past main verb.

Cum essem ōtiōsus in Tusculānō, accēpī tuās litterās.
While I was at leisure at my house in Tusculum, I received your letter. Cicero, Ad Fam. 9.18

Wishes that are not true now

A&G §441

The imperfect subjunctive wishes that the present were different, and implies that it is not. Utinam regularly comes first. English uses "if only" or "I wish" with a past form.

Clue: Utinam with an imperfect subjunctive.

Utinam adesset!
If only he were here!

Possibility in the past

A&G §446

The imperfect subjunctive can say what could have been the case in the past, often with "you" meaning anyone. English uses "would have" or "could have."

Clue: An imperfect subjunctive main verb, often 2nd person singular, with no signal word.

Crēderēs victōs.
You would have thought them defeated.

Conditions that are not true now

A&G §517

In "if" sentences about the present, the imperfect subjunctive in both halves says that the condition is not true. English uses "if ... were" and "would."

Clue: Sī with an imperfect subjunctive in both halves of the sentence.

Sī adessēs, gaudērem.
If you were here, I would be glad.
