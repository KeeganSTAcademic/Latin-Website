Perfect Active Indicative

The perfect describes either a completed action in the present ("he has loved") or a simple action in the past ("he loved"). The complete present is for an action that is finished by the time of speaking, like "I have arrived!" from someone who just showed up, or one finished earlier that still affects the present: you could say "I have studied two years of Latin" now or ten years from now. The simple past says only that an action happened; it is neither complete nor incomplete (see below for more).

The forms

Irregular: sum, esse (to be)

sum is irregular in the present system, but its perfect is completely regular: the stem fu- plus the perfect endings, fuī, fuistī, fuit, fuimus, fuistis, fuērunt.

How to build it

Take the 3rd principal part and drop the -ī to get the complete stem. The 3rd principal part is the base for every complete tense in the active, the way the 2nd principal part is the base for the incomplete ones. The perfect has its own set of endings: -ī, -istī, -it, -imus, -istis, -ērunt.

How the 3rd principal part is formed advanced

The 3rd principal part has to be learned with each verb, but most are made in one of five ways (A&G §177). Each conjugation tends to prefer one of them, so knowing the patterns helps you learn new verbs faster and recognize a perfect you have not learned yet.

  1. Add -v- or -u- after the stem: amāvī, monuī.
  2. Add -s- to the root. A c, g or h before it combines with the s and is written x: rēxī from regō.
  3. Repeat the first consonant with a vowel in front of the root (reduplication): cecidī from cadō.
  4. Lengthen the root vowel, which sometimes also changes it: lēgī from legō, ēgī from agō.
  5. Change nothing: the root stays as it is and only the ending shows the tense: vertī from vertō.

1st conjugation

Almost always -āvī: amō → amāvī, vocō → vocāvī, laudō → laudāvī. A new 1st-conjugation verb is a safe bet to follow it.

Other patterns:

  • -uī secō → secuī, domō → domuī, vetō → vetuī, sonō → sonuī, cubō → cubuī
  • Reduplication dō → dedī, stō → stetī
  • Lengthened vowel iuvō → iūvī, lavō → lāvī

2nd conjugation

Usually -uī: moneō → monuī, habeō → habuī, terreō → terruī, doceō → docuī. The ē of the stem drops out.

Other patterns:

  • -ēvī dēleō → dēlēvī, fleō → flēvī, impleō → implēvī
  • -sī or -xī maneō → mānsī, rīdeō → rīsī, iubeō → iussī, augeō → auxī, suādeō → suāsī
  • Lengthened vowel videō → vīdī, sedeō → sēdī, moveō → mōvī, caveō → cāvī
  • Reduplication mordeō → momordī, pendeō → pependī, spondeō → spopondī

3rd conjugation

There is no usual pattern; the 3rd conjugation uses all five. Learn each perfect with the verb. The groups below help you recognize one you have forgotten.

The five patterns:

  • -sī or -xī regō → rēxī, dīcō → dīxī, dūcō → dūxī, scrībō → scrīpsī, mittō → mīsī, gerō → gessī, vīvō → vīxī
  • Reduplication currō → cucurrī, cadō → cecidī, tangō → tetigī, pellō → pepulī, discō → didicī
  • Lengthened vowel legō → lēgī, emō → ēmī, agō → ēgī, vincō → vīcī, relinquō → relīquī, frangō → frēgī
  • Unchanged vertō → vertī, dēfendō → dēfendī, ostendō → ostendī, solvō → solvī, bibō → bibī, metuō → metuī
  • -vī or -uī petō → petīvī, quaerō → quaesīvī, nōscō → nōvī, pōnō → posuī, colō → coluī

In the reduplicated perfects the root vowel often weakens: cadō has cecidī, not *cecadī.

3rd conjugation -iō

These behave like the 3rd conjugation. The common ones mostly lengthen the vowel, and often change it to ē: capiō → cēpī, faciō → fēcī, iaciō → iēcī, fugiō → fūgī.

Other patterns:

  • -īvī or -uī cupiō → cupīvī, rapiō → rapuī
  • -sī or -xī aspiciō → aspexī
  • Reduplication pariō → peperī

4th conjugation

Usually -īvī: audiō → audīvī, mūniō → mūnīvī, dormiō → dormīvī, sciō → scīvī.

Other patterns:

  • Lengthened vowel veniō → vēnī
  • -uī aperiō → aperuī, saliō → saluī
  • -sī or -xī sentiō → sēnsī, hauriō → hausī, vinciō → vīnxī
  • Reduplication reperiō → repperī

Shortened forms: -v- perfects are often shortened, especially in poetry. Amāvistī can appear as amāstī, audīvit as audiit, and amāvērunt as amārunt.

Watch the lengthened-vowel perfects: venit (he comes) and vēnit (he came) differ only in the length of the e, which most texts do not mark. Context, and other verbs in the sentence, usually tell you which one it is.

The basic use: choosing the English translation A&G §473

In a story or a report of events, use the simple past: "Caesar came, saw and conquered." When someone is talking about the situation now, and the result of the action still matters, use "has ___ed": "I have written the letter" (so here it is).

How to spot it: A complete stem such as amāv-, monu- or rēx- shows a complete tense. Several perfect endings look like present endings, so check the stem before the ending: amāvit, not amat.

Epistulam scrīpsī.
I have written the letter. (It is finished.)
Caesar vēnit, vīdit, vīcit.
Caesar came, saw and conquered.

Has loved or loved?

The perfect can be hard to understand at first, because English grammar rarely talks about aspect: whether an action is finished, separate from when it happens. Even so, we use the difference every day.

Imagine that you are late to work, and your boss calls just after you walk into the building. You would say "I have arrived," because you want to stress both that the arriving is done (complete) and that it affects the present (you are currently there). "I arrive" or "I am arriving" would imply that you haven't gotten there yet, while "I arrived" or "I had arrived" would suggest that you might no longer be there.

Latin uses the perfect for both "I have arrived" and "I arrived." The context tells you which one fits.

Other uses

A perfect with a present meaning

A&G §476

A few verbs use the perfect to describe a present state. Nōvī literally means "I have learned," so it is translated "I know." Meminī means "I remember," and ōdī means "I hate."

Clue: Translate nōvī, meminī, ōdī and cōnsuēvī with the English present tense.

Tē nōvī.
I know you.
