The perfect tense

The Latin perfect tense describes either complete action that occurs now or simple action that occurred in the past. The completed present time translates like "he has loved," while the secondary simple past translates like "he loved." Translating the perfect can be difficult due to this ambiguity and often depends on context.

The perfect is formed by adding the perfect endings to the 3rd principal part. These endings are quite similar to the incomplete endings in everything but the 1st (-ī) and 2nd singular (-istī). On one hand, this makes them easier to learn, but it also means that students must pay close attention to the stem to distinguish the perfect from the present tense. Note that the 3rd principal part is used for all active verbs with complete action in Latin, much in the same way the 2nd principal part is used for all incomplete tenses.

Has loved or loved?

The complete present can be difficult to get one's head around, as the idea of aspect (an action's completion being separate from time) is rarely broached in traditional English grammar. However, it is a distinction we organically make in our day-to-day speech.

Imagine that you are late to work and receive a call from your boss just after you entered the building. You would tell your boss "I have arrived" because you want them to know that you just finished arriving and are at work. Telling them "I am arriving" would imply you are still running up to the door or perhaps pulling into the car park. Telling them "I arrived" would imply you already arrived at some past point in time and perhaps something else happened to make you leave work.

Irregular: sum, esse (to be)

sum is irregular in the present system, but its perfect is completely regular: the stem fu- plus the perfect endings, fuī, fuistī, fuit, fuimus, fuistis, fuērunt.
