The Subjunctive: Overview

the mood for actions that are wished, possible, intended or reportedA&G §§154, 438

Every Latin verb has a mood. The indicative presents an action as a fact: venit, "he comes." The subjunctive presents it in some other way. Its meanings come from two old ideas: will, what someone wants to happen ("let him come," "may he come"), and imagining, what could happen ("he would come"). Most of its uses grow out of one or the other.

English has almost lost its subjunctive. It survives in a few phrases ("if I were you," "God save the king"); otherwise English uses helping words such as may, might, would and let, or an ordinary verb. So the Latin subjunctive has no single translation. Students often find this the hardest part: you translate a subjunctive according to its use, and you need to find the use first.

Where you find it A&G §438

In main clauses

The subjunctive is the main verb.

It adds an attitude to the action: an encouragement, a command, a wish, a doubtful question or a possibility. These uses are less common, and English usually needs let, may or should.

Amēmus. Let us love.Quid faciam? What am I to do?

In dependent clauses

Something in the sentence calls for it.

Most subjunctives are here, after words such as ut, nē, cum and quī, or in a reported question. These clauses began as main-clause subjunctives attached to another sentence: mīsit lēgātōs quī dīcerent first meant "he sent envoys; they were to say," and came to mean "he sent envoys to say." As the clause became a fixed construction, the subjunctive's own meaning faded, so English usually translates it with an ordinary verb.

Vēnit ut vidēret. He came to see.Rogāvit quid facerem. He asked what I was doing.

Every use is listed, with how to spot it, on Uses of the Subjunctive.

The four tenses

The subjunctive has four tenses: two incomplete and two complete, built from the same principal parts as the indicative. It has no future. For a later action Latin uses the incomplete tenses, or, where it must be clear, the future participle with sim or essem: prōspiciō quī concursūs futūrī sint, "I foresee what crowds there will be."

Sequence of tenses A&G §§482–485

In a dependent clause, the main verb decides which subjunctive tense to use. This pattern is called sequence of tenses. It is a strong tendency rather than a strict law, and writers sometimes break it for emphasis. To choose the tense, ask two questions.

1. When is the main verb? A present or future main verb puts the sentence in primary sequence, which uses the present or perfect subjunctive. A past main verb puts it in secondary sequence, which uses the imperfect or pluperfect subjunctive: scrībit ut nōs moneat, "he writes to warn us," but scrīpsit ut nōs monēret, "he wrote to warn us." A perfect main verb usually counts as past ("he loved"); when the writer has the present in mind ("has loved"), it can take primary sequence.

2. When is the dependent action, compared with the main verb? If it happens at the same time or later, use an incomplete tense (present or imperfect). If it happened earlier, use a complete tense (perfect or pluperfect).

Rogō quid faciās.I ask what you are doing.primary · same time → present
Rogō quid fēcerīs.I ask what you did.primary · earlier → perfect
Rogāvī quid facerēs.I asked what you were doing.secondary · same time → imperfect
Rogāvī quid fēcissēs.I asked what you had done.secondary · earlier → pluperfect
Translate by the sequence, not with "may" and "might"

In rogāvī quid facerēs, the imperfect subjunctive is simply "you were doing." The helping words may and might belong to a few uses, such as possibility and some purpose clauses. In most dependent clauses, use the English tense that the sequence points to.
