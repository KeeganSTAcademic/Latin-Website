Present Subjunctive

The present subjunctive is the incomplete subjunctive of primary sequence. In a main clause it gives an encouragement, a command, a doubtful question or a possibility: amēmus, "let us love." In a dependent clause after a present or future main verb, it describes an action at the same time as the main verb or later: rogō quid amet, "I ask what he loves." It has no single English translation; the use decides.

For what the subjunctive is and how sequence of tenses works, see The Subjunctive: Overview. Every use is listed on Uses of the Subjunctive.

The forms

Irregular: sum, esse (to be)

sum uses i instead of e or a: sim, sīs, sit, sīmus, sītis, sint. possum follows it: possim, possit.

How to build it

Change the vowel after the stem, then add the personal endings. In the 1st conjugation the ā of the stem becomes e (amā- → ame-). The other conjugations put a after the stem (monea-, rega-, capia-, audia-). The 1st singular ends in -m in the active and -r in the passive, as in the imperfect indicative. Many students remember the vowels with the phrase "we fear a liar": e for the 1st conjugation, ea for the 2nd, a for the 3rd, ia for the 3rd -iō and 4th.

The basic use: after a present or future main verb A&G §483

In a dependent clause after a present or future main verb, the present subjunctive shows an action at the same time as the main verb or later. Translate it with an ordinary English present or future, not with "may": rogō quid faciat, "I ask what he is doing."

How to spot it: Look for the changed vowel before the ending: e in the 1st conjugation (amet), a in the others (moneat, regat, capiat, audiat).

Rogō quid faciās.
I ask what you are doing.
Scrībit ut nōs moneat.
He writes to warn us.
Nescit cūr puella lacrimet.
He doesn't know why the girl is crying.

Other uses

Encouragements and commands

A&G §439

This is the subjunctive's oldest meaning: the speaker's will. The 1st person plural encourages ("let us ..."), and the 3rd person gives a command ("let him ...," "he is to ..."). The negative is nē.

Clue: A present subjunctive is the main verb, often at the start of the sentence.

Vīvāmus, mea Lesbia, atque amēmus.
Let us live, my Lesbia, and let us love. Catullus 5.1
Nē timeant.
Let them not be afraid.

Doubtful questions

A&G §444

A question in the subjunctive asks what should be done. It began as a request to learn someone's will ("what do you want me to do?") and came to express doubt, frustration or impossibility. English uses "am I to" or "should I." The negative is nōn.

Clue: A question whose main verb is subjunctive, usually in the 1st person.

Quid agam, iūdicēs?
What am I to do, judges?

Possibility

A&G §446

The subjunctive can present an action as something that could happen, not as a fact or a wish. English uses "may," "might," "would" or "could," whichever fits. The negative is nōn.

Clue: A subjunctive main verb, often with an indefinite subject such as aliquis, or with "you" meaning anyone.

Dīcat aliquis.
Someone may say.
Fortūnam citius reperiās quam retineās.
You could find good fortune sooner than you could keep it.

Wishes that may come true

A&G §441

The present subjunctive wishes for something that can still happen. It may have utinam; the negative is nē. English uses "may" or "I hope that."

Clue: Utinam, or a wish such as "may the gods ..." with a present subjunctive.

Dī tē servent!
May the gods keep you safe!
