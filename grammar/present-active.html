The present tense describes what is happening now, or what is generally true. Latin has one form where English has three: amat can be translated "she loves," "she is loving" or "she does love."

The forms

Irregular: sum, esse (to be)

The most common verb in Latin doesn't belong to any conjugation, but its endings are the same personal endings you see above, with -m instead of -ō for "I." Learn it early: you'll need it for sentences like puella est bona.

How to build it

Cut -re off the infinitive, the 2nd principal part, to get the incomplete stem (amā-, monē-), then add the personal ending, which tells you who is doing the action. The ending must agree with the subject. These endings are used throughout the verb system, so they are worth memorizing first.

In the 3rd conjugation the short linking vowel -e- changes to -i- in most forms and to -u- in the 3rd plural: regis, regit; regunt. The Romans seem simply to have preferred the sound. The change is inconvenient, but it is worth learning now, because later it is how you tell the present (regit) from the future (reget).

The basic use: choosing the English translation A&G §465

Choose the English form that fits the sentence. Use "she loves" for a habit or something generally true, "she is loving" for something going on at this moment, and "she does love" for emphasis.

Questions and negatives: In questions and negative sentences, English usually needs "do": nōn amat, "she does not love"; amatne?, "does she love?"

Puella cantat.
The girl sings. / The girl is singing.
Nautae nōn nāvigant.
The sailors do not sail. / The sailors are not sailing.
Tē amō.
I love you.

Other uses

An action continuing from the past

A&G §466

With iam diū ("for a long time now") or iam prīdem, the present describes an action that began in the past and is still going on. English uses "has been ___ing" for this.

Clue: Look for iam diū, iam prīdem or a length of time next to a present verb.

Iam diū tē exspectō.
I have been waiting for you for a long time.

An attempted action

A&G §467

Because the present shows an action that is not finished, it can describe an attempt. In that case, translate it as "is trying to ___."

Clue: The context shows that the action has not succeeded, or cannot have succeeded yet.

Hostēs urbem capiunt.
The enemy are trying to take the city.

The historical present

A&G §469

When telling a story about the past, Latin writers often switch to the present tense to make the events feel vivid. Translate these verbs with the English past tense.

Clue: Present-tense verbs appear in the middle of a story that is otherwise told in the past.

Caesar flūmen trānsit.
Caesar crossed the river. (literally: crosses)
