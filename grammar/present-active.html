The present tense

The Latin present tense active indicative describes incomplete action happening now. We translate it either like "he loves" (simple present), "he is loving" (an ongoing present), or "he does love" (an emphatic present).

It is formed from the incomplete stem, which you get by cutting the -re off the second principal part, and attaching the personal endings matching the person and number of the sentence's subject. These endings must agree with the subject in the nominative if there is one, and they should be one of the first things you memorize, as they recur throughout the entire verbal system.

Note that in the 3rd conjugation the expected short -e- linking vowel changes to a short -i- in most forms and to a -u- in the 3rd plural (regis, regit; regunt). Linguists think this shift was "euphonic": essentially, the Romans liked the sound of it better. Though annoying, this change is important to learn now, because later it will be a critical piece of information in telling the present apart from other tenses and moods.

What is a conjugation?

Latin verbs change their endings to show who is doing the action. The ending does the job of an English pronoun: amat already means "he (or she, or it) is loving," so Latin often leaves the subject out.

A conjugation is a family of verbs that share a stem vowel. You find it in the infinitive, the second word in the dictionary entry: drop the -re and look at the vowel that's left (A&G §§171, 175).

What the present means. The present tense describes an action that is going on right now. At heart, amat means "she is loving." English often uses its simple present for the same idea, so amat can also be translated "she loves." Choose whichever sounds natural in the sentence. (A&G §163)

Irregular: sum, esse (to be)

The most common verb in Latin doesn't belong to any conjugation, but its endings are the same personal endings you see above, with -m instead of -ō for "I." Learn it early: you'll need it for sentences like puella est bona.
