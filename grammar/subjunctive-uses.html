Uses of the Subjunctive

every common use, and how to spot itA&G §§439–580

The subjunctive appears in two places: as the main verb of a sentence, where it adds a wish, a command, a doubt or a possibility, and in dependent clauses, where a word such as ut, nē, cum or quī, or a reported question, calls for it.

To translate a subjunctive, first find what calls for it, then use the translation that goes with that use. The tense follows sequence of tenses, explained on The Subjunctive: Overview.

In main clauses

Encouragements and commands

A&G §439

The oldest meaning of the subjunctive is the speaker's will. The 1st person plural encourages ("let us ..."); the 3rd person gives a command ("let him ..."). The negative is nē.

Clue: A present subjunctive as the main verb.

Eāmus!
Let's go!
Nē timeant.
Let them not be afraid.

Granting a point

A&G §440

The same use can grant something for the sake of argument, before the speaker makes the real point. English uses "grant that" or "suppose." The negative is nē.

Clue: A present or perfect subjunctive as the main verb, followed by a "but" or "still."

Sit fūr, sit sacrilegus.
Grant that he is a thief and a temple robber.

Prohibitions

A&G §450

Latin forbids an action in three ways: nōlī with an infinitive (the usual way), cavē with the present subjunctive, or nē with the perfect subjunctive, which is blunter. The perfect here does not refer to the past.

Clue: Nē with a 2nd person perfect subjunctive, or cavē with a present subjunctive.

Nē timueris.
Don't be afraid.
Cavē festīnēs.
Don't hurry.

Wishes

A&G §441

The tense shows whether the wish can still come true. The present wishes for something possible; the imperfect wishes the present were different; the pluperfect wishes the past had been different. Utinam regularly comes before the imperfect and pluperfect. The negative is nē.

Clue: Utinam, or a wish such as "may the gods ..."

Utinam veniat!
I hope he comes!
Utinam adesset!
If only he were here!
Utinam adfuisset!
If only he had been here!

Doubtful questions

A&G §444

A question in the subjunctive asks what should be done. It began as a request to learn someone's will and came to express doubt, frustration or impossibility. English uses "am I to" or "should I." The negative is nōn.

Clue: A question whose main verb is subjunctive, usually 1st person.

Quid agam, iūdicēs?
What am I to do, judges?
Quid facerem?
What was I to do?

Possibility

A&G §446

The subjunctive presents an action as something that could happen, not as a fact or a wish. English uses "may," "might," "would" or "could," whichever fits. The present looks to the future; the imperfect looks to the past. The negative is nōn.

Clue: A subjunctive main verb, often with an indefinite subject such as aliquis, or with "you" meaning anyone.

Dīcat aliquis.
Someone may say.
Crēderēs victōs.
You would have thought them defeated.

In dependent clauses

Purpose

A&G §531

Ut (negative nē) with the subjunctive says why someone does something. English often uses an infinitive ("to," "in order to"), but Latin prose never does: it uses a clause.

Clue: Ut or nē after a verb of action, with no signpost word such as tam or ita.

Ab arātrō abdūxērunt Cincinnātum, ut dictātor esset.
They brought Cincinnatus from the plow to be dictator.
Fūgit nē caperētur.
He fled so that he would not be captured.

Relative clause of purpose

A&G §531

A relative pronoun or adverb can introduce a purpose clause, especially when the purpose belongs to one word in the sentence. English uses "to."

Clue: Quī, quae, quod with a subjunctive, often after a verb of sending.

Mittitur quī locī nātūram perspiciat.
He is sent to examine the lay of the land.

Indirect command

A&G §563

Verbs whose action points to the future (asking, ordering, urging, warning, persuading, permitting) take a clause with ut or nē and the subjunctive. It works like a purpose clause that is the object of the verb. English uses "to" or "not to." Iubeō and vetō take an infinitive instead.

Clue: Ut or nē after a verb such as rogō, ōrō, imperō, moneō, hortor, persuādeō.

Monet ut omnēs suspīciōnēs vītet.
He warns him to avoid all suspicion.
Mē ōrat nē discēdam.
She begs me not to leave.

Result

A&G §537

Ut (negative ut nōn) with the subjunctive gives the result of something described in the main clause. English uses "that," or sometimes an infinitive: "too far away to see."

Clue: A signpost word in the main clause: tam, ita, sīc, adeō ("so"), tantus ("so great"), tālis ("such").

Tanta vīs probitātis est ut eam in hoste dīligāmus.
Goodness has such power that we love it even in an enemy. Cicero, Laelius 29
Tam longē aberam ut nōn vidērem.
I was too far away to see.

Relative clause of characteristic

A&G §535

A relative clause in the indicative states a fact about a particular person or thing. In the subjunctive it says what kind of person or thing is meant. English uses "the kind of ... who," "such as" or an ordinary verb.

Clue: Sunt quī, nēmō est quī, quis est quī, sōlus quī, or dignus quī.

Nēmō est quī hoc nesciat.
There is no one who does not know this.
Sunt quī dīcant.
There are people who say so.

Indirect question

A&G §574

A question opened by a question word can be part of a larger sentence, as the object of a verb of asking, knowing, telling or doubting. Its verb is subjunctive, and English uses an ordinary verb.

Clue: A question word (quis, quid, cūr, ubi, quandō, num, -ne, utrum) after a verb such as rogō, sciō, nesciō, dīcō.

Quid ipse sentiam expōnam.
I will explain what I think.
Rogāvit quis vēnisset.
He asked who had come.

Fearing

A&G §564

After verbs of fearing (timeō, metuō, vereor), nē means "that" and ut means "that ... not." This makes sense once you see where it comes from: timeō nē veniat began as two sentences, "I am afraid. May he not come!"

Clue: A verb of fearing followed by nē or ut.

Timeō nē Verrēs fēcerit.
I am afraid that Verres has done it.
Vereor ut tibi possim concēdere.
I am afraid that I cannot grant you this.

Cum clauses

A&G §546

Cum with the indicative dates an action. With the subjunctive it describes the circumstances ("when," "while"), or gives a reason ("since") or a concession ("although," A&G §549). Tamen in the main clause points to "although."

Clue: Cum followed by a subjunctive.

Cum id nūntiātum esset, mātūrat.
When this had been reported, he hurries. Caesar, B.G. 1.7
Cum prīmī ōrdinēs concidissent, tamen ācerrimē reliquī resistēbant.
Although the front ranks had fallen, the rest still resisted fiercely. Caesar, B.G. 7.62

Conditions

A&G §516–517

A condition in the subjunctive is imagined, not stated as a fact. The present subjunctive imagines a possible future ("should ... would"). The imperfect and pluperfect imagine something that is not true now or was not true then, so they imply the opposite.

Clue: Sī or nisi with a subjunctive in both halves of the sentence.

Sī rogēs Aristōnem, neget.
If you should ask Aristo, he would say no.
Sī adessēs, gaudērem.
If you were here, I would be glad.
Sī vēnissēs, tē vīdissem.
If you had come, I would have seen you.

Clauses inside indirect statement

A&G §580

When a statement is reported with an infinitive, the verbs of its dependent clauses become subjunctive. English uses an ordinary verb.

Clue: A dependent clause inside an accusative-and-infinitive statement.

Dīxit puerum, quī adesset, fessum esse.
He said that the boy who was present was tired.
Purpose or result?

In positive sentences the two look the same. A result clause usually follows a signpost word in the main clause (tam, ita, sīc, tantus). The negative tells them apart: purpose uses nē, result uses ut nōn. Compare custōdītus est nē effugeret, "he was guarded so that he would not escape" (purpose), with custōdītus est ut nōn effugeret, "he was guarded, so that he did not escape" (result).
