Introduction to Verbs

a word for an action or a stateA&G §§153–176

A verb says what happens, or what something is: amat (she loves), currit (he runs), est (it is). Almost every sentence has one, and in Latin it usually comes at the end.

An English verb often needs several words: they were being loved. Latin usually says the same thing in one word, amābantur, so each Latin verb form carries a lot of information. Two parts of the word are reliable guides in every tense. The personal ending tells you the person and number: who is doing the action, and how many. The stem tells you the aspect: whether the action is incomplete or complete. Other information, such as time, mood and voice, is marked in different ways depending on the form.

What a verb tells you A&G §§154, 157

Every form of a Latin verb answers five questions at once.

Person

Who is doing it?

The 1st person is the speaker (I, we); the 2nd person is the one spoken to (you); the 3rd person is anyone or anything else (he, she, it, they). The personal ending shows it, so Latin usually leaves out the subject pronoun: amat by itself means he loves or she loves.

amō: I loveamās: you loveamat: he, she or it loves

Number

One, or more than one?

The verb is singular or plural, and it must agree with its subject. The personal ending shows number and person together.

puella amat: the girl lovespuellae amant: the girls love

Tense

When, and is it finished?

A tense combines a time (past, present or future) with an aspect (incomplete or complete). Both are explained below.

amābat: he was lovingamāverat: he had loved

Voice

Does the subject do it, or receive it?

In the active voice the subject performs the action; in the passive it receives it. English needs extra words for the passive (is loved, has been loved). Latin uses passive personal endings in the incomplete tenses (amātur) and the perfect participle with a form of sum in the complete ones (amāta est).

puella amat: the girl lovespuella amātur: the girl is loved

Mood

A fact, a command, or something else?

The indicative states facts and asks questions. The imperative gives commands. The subjunctive is used for things that are wished, possible or intended, and in many kinds of clause; English often uses may, might or would for it. The subjunctive has its own pages, starting with The Subjunctive: Overview.

amat: he loves (indicative)amā!: love! (imperative)

Tense: time and aspect A&G §§154.c, 160–162

Time is when the action takes place: in the past, now, or in the future. In the indicative, the past tenses tend to have an a before the ending (amābat, amāverat) and the future tenses an i (amābit, amāverit). English has a similar pattern in its helping verbs: was for the past, will for the future.

Aspect is how the action is viewed: as incomplete (in progress, repeated, or not yet finished) or as complete (finished). He was writing and he had written are both in the past; they differ only in aspect. Whether an action is ongoing or finished is a separate question from when it happens: I have arrived describes an action completed in the present, I had arrived an action completed in the past. Latin shows aspect with the stem. The incomplete tenses, active and passive, are built on the incomplete stem (amā-, from the second principal part). The complete tenses are built on a complete stem: amāv- (from the third principal part) in the active, and the perfect participle amātus (from the fourth) with a form of sum in the passive.

Person and number: the personal endings A&G §§163, 165

Principal parts and the four conjugations A&G §§171–172

Every verb is learned with its four principal parts, because together they give you every stem the verb uses. A conjugation is a family of verbs that share a stem vowel. You find it in the infinitive, the second principal part: drop the -re and look at the vowel that is left.

4thaudiō, audīre, hearī-iō and -īre
Telling the conjugations apart

A first principal part in -eō always means the 2nd conjugation. One in -iō could be 3rd -iō or 4th, so check the infinitive: capere (short e) or audīre (long ī). The 1st and 3rd conjugations both have -ō, so again the infinitive decides: amāre or regere.
