The future perfect tense

The Latin future perfect tense describes complete action that will occur at some later time. We translate it like "he will have loved." Its formation is incredibly simple: we attach the future of the verb sum (erō, eris, erit …) to the perfect stem.

This is a way of marking time very similar to the -bi- seen in the future tense, with the same -i- as a time vowel. We may understand the future perfect as the combination of a stem communicating completed aspect, an -i- vowel marking future time, and personal endings marking the person and number of a verb. Note that the expected -erunt (the future of sum) becomes -erint in the 3rd plural.

Will love or will have loved?

The future perfect can be odd to translate, but we may essentially think of it as a much more definite future tense. If I tell you that "I will be grading" (an incomplete future) on the weekend, I am making a much more wishy-washy claim than if I say that "I will have graded" (a complete future) on the weekend.

A very common use of the future perfect is delivering prophecy.
