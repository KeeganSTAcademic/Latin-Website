Overview

How a Latin verb carries its meaning

An English verb spreads its information across several words. In they were being loved, the pronoun they tells us who is involved, were tells us the action is in the past and that more than one person is involved, being tells us the action was in progress, and being loved tells us the subject received the action rather than performed it.

Latin puts all of this into a single word, amābantur, and it always puts each piece of information in the same position. The front of the word (the stem) gives the verb's meaning and its aspect. The middle (the time marker) says when the action happens. The end (the personal ending) says who does it, how many, and whether they are doing it or having it done to them. Reading a Latin verb is a matter of taking the word apart in that order.

The five pieces of information

Aspect stem
Aspect is how the action is viewed: as incomplete (in progress, repeated, or not yet finished) or as complete (finished). He was writing and he had written are both in the past; they differ only in aspect. Whether an action is ongoing or finished is a separate question from when it happens: I have arrived describes an action completed in the present, I had arrived an action completed in the past. Latin communicates aspect through verb stems. The incomplete stem (amā-, from the 2nd principal part) is used for incomplete action; the complete stem (amāv-, from the 3rd principal part) for complete action.
Time marker
Time is when the action takes place: in the past, now, or in the future. Latin shows it with a marker between stem and ending, built on a for the past and i for the future, with no marker for the present. What we call a tense (imperfect, future perfect, and so on) is a combination of aspect and time. Two aspects times three times gives Latin its six indicative tenses: the present, imperfect, and future (incomplete), and the perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect (complete). English does something similar with its helping verbs: the vowel of was lines up with the a of the past marker, and the vowel of will with the i of the later marker.
Person ending
The personal ending determines both person and number. Person tells who performs the action in relation to the speaker. The 1st person is the speaker (I, we); the 2nd person is the one spoken to (you, y'all); the 3rd person is anyone or anything else (he, she, it, they). Because the ending already names the person, Latin usually leaves out the subject pronoun: amat by itself means "he loves" or "she loves."
Number ending
Number tells whether the subject is one (singular) or more than one (plural). The verb must agree with its subject in number: puella amat "the girl loves," puellae amant "the girls love."
Voice ending
Voice tells whether the subject performs the action (active: puella amat "the girl loves") or receives it (passive: puella amātur "the girl is loved"). English needs a helping verb and a participle for the passive (is loved, was being loved, will be loved). Latin changes only the ending, usually by adding an r. In the incomplete tenses, voice is determined by the ending alone.

Person, number, and voice share the last slot, so a single ending answers three questions at once. -ntur means 3rd person, plural, passive; -mus means 1st person, plural, active.

Incomplete · present time

The present

English is / are (no time marker)

Take the incomplete stem (amā-re → amā-) and add the ending for person and number. There is no time marker; the present is the default form.

Translations
  • amat: he loves · he is loving · he does love
  • amātur: he is loved · he is being loved
  • With iam diū: "has been loving" §466
  • In narrative, a vivid past: "he loves" = "he loved" §469
Incomplete · past time

The imperfect

English was / were
amābat

Take the incomplete stem (amā-re → amā-), add the very distinctive infix -ba-, then the ending for person and number. The a as a past-time marker appears again in the pluperfect: amāv-er-a-m.

Translations
  • amābat: he was loving · he used to love · he kept loving
  • He began to love · he tried to love §471
  • For states and description, a plain past: "he loved" §§470–471
  • amābātur: he was being loved · he used to be loved
Incomplete · future time

The future

English will / will be verbing
amābit

In the 1st (-āre) and 2nd (-ēre) conjugations, take the incomplete stem (amā-re → amā-), add the infix -bi-, then the ending for person and number. The i as a future-time marker appears again in the future perfect: amāv-er-i-t. The 3rd and 4th conjugations use a vowel instead (§7).

Translations
  • amābit: he will love · he will be loving · (older) he shall love
  • amābitur: he will be loved
  • Often "loves" in English after if or when: sī amābit "if he loves" §472

    §1

    Structure of the finite verb

    A finite verb in the incomplete system is read in three slots, left to right:

    1. The stem carries the aspect: whether the action is presented as ongoing (incomplete) or finished (complete). It includes the conjugation's stem vowel.
    2. The time marker carries the tense proper: past, present, or future.
    3. The ending carries person, number, and voice.

    Thus amābitis = y'all will love. Allen & Greenough divide the verb into stem and ending, with the ending made up of a "mood or time marker" plus a personal ending. The time marker here is their tense sign.

    §2

    Aspect: the two stems

    1. The incomplete stem is found by dropping -re from the present infinitive: amāre → amā-, monēre → monē-, regere → rege-, audīre → audī-. It forms the three incomplete tenses: present, imperfect, future.
    2. The complete stem (3rd principal part minus -ī: amāvī → amāv-) forms the three complete tenses: perfect, pluperfect, future perfect (active).
    3. The stem is chosen before anything else. Once the stem tells you "ongoing" or "finished," the marker only has to add the time.
    See A&G §160 Continued vs. completed action A&G §§174–176 Present stem A&G §§171–173 Conjugations
    §3

    Time: the markers

    Both systems mark time the same way: a marker with a for past time, a marker with i for future time, and no marker for present time.

    1. Incomplete markers: -bā- (imperfect), -bi- (future, 1st & 2nd conj.; see §6–7 for the others).
    2. Complete markers: -erā- (pluperfect), -eri- (future perfect).
    3. English uses the same slot and, by coincidence, the same vowels: was / will.

    Parallel irregularities. The 1st singular future drops the i before -ō in both systems: amābō, amāverō. In the 3rd plural the future perfect keeps it (amāverint), while the future changes it to u (amābunt).

    §4

    The unmarked tenses: present and perfect

    1. The present and perfect carry no time marker. Each is the default "now" of its system, identified only by its stem and endings: amat, amāvit.
    2. The perfect's primary sense is the complete present (A&G's perfect definite): an action finished as of now. amāvit "he has loved."
    3. Its secondary sense is the simple past (historical perfect): amāvit "he loved." This is the usual sense in narrative.

    §5

    Person, number, and voice: the endings

    The passive endings are the active endings with an r added or substituted. The 2nd plural -minī is the only exception.

    PersonActivePassive 1st sg · I-ō / -m→-or / -r 2nd sg · you-s→-ris 3rd sg · he/she/it-t→-tur 1st pl · we-mus→-mur 2nd pl · y'all-tis→-minī 3rd pl · they-nt→-ntur
    1. 1st sg -ō / -or is used in the present and in the -bō future; -m / -r after a marker in a: amābam, regam.
    2. A long vowel shortens before -m (-r), -t, -nt (-ntur): amābās but amābat; audīs but audit.

    Future of the 1st and 2nd conjugations: -bi-

    §7

    Future of the 3rd, 3rd -iō, and 4th conjugations: the vowel shift

    1. These conjugations do not use -bi-. The future is marked by a vowel: -a- in the 1st singular, -ē- in all other persons (shortened by §5.2). In origin these are subjunctive forms used with future meaning.
    2. These future vowels are a vowel shift, not the a/i time pattern seen throughout the rest of the indicative (§3). The -a- of regam does not mark the past, and the -ē- of regēs is not the future i.
    3. The 1st sg (regam, audiar) has an a and an m/r ending and can be mistaken for a past form.
    4. Because the marker is a single vowel, several futures look like present forms of another conjugation. The conjugation must be known from the dictionary entry.
    regēsyou will rule · 3rd, future
    regisyou rule · 3rd, present
    monēsyou warn · 2nd, present
    audiēsyou will hear · 4th, future

    §8

    The stem vowel of the 3rd conjugation

    1. The 3rd conjugation's stem vowel is short e/o (seen in regere), which appears as i inside the word and u before -nt: regis, regit, regunt.
    2. It stays e in the 2nd sg present passive: regeris. Compare the future regēris, which differs only in vowel length.
    3. 3rd -iō and 4th verbs keep their i throughout and show iu in the 3rd plural: capiunt, audiunt.
    4. In the imperfect the vowel is ē (3rd) or iē (3rd -iō, 4th): regēbam, capiēbam, audiēbam.
