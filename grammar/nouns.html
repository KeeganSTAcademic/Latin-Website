Introduction to Nouns

a person, place, thing or ideaA&G §§20–22, 29–38

A noun is the name of a person, place, thing or idea: puella (girl), Rōma (Rome), equus (horse), pāx (peace). A noun that names one particular person or place, like Rōma or Caesar, is a proper noun and starts with a capital letter. All other nouns are common nouns.

Latin nouns work differently from English ones. In English, a noun's position in the sentence tells you its function. "The dog bites the man" and "The man bites the dog" use the same words, and only the order changes who does what. Latin shows a noun's function with the ending on the noun instead. Changing the end of a word to show its function is called inflection (A&G §21). Because the endings carry the meaning, a Latin writer can put the words in almost any order, and uses the order to show emphasis.

What every noun has A&G §§22, 29–35

The ending of a Latin noun tells you three things at once. When you read a noun, these are the three questions to ask.

Case

What is its function in the sentence?

The case is the form that shows a noun's function: whether it is the subject, the object, the owner of something, and so on. Latin has six main cases, listed below.

puella: the girl (as the subject)puellam: the girl (as the object)

Number

One, or more than one?

A singular noun means one; a plural noun means more than one. Each case has a singular and a plural form, so number and case are shown by the same ending.

puella: the girlpuellae: the girls

Gender

Masculine, feminine or neuter?

Every noun belongs to one of three genders. For people, gender usually matches whether the person is male or female. For things, gender is a grammar category and has nothing to do with being male or female: mēnsa (table) is feminine and bellum (war) is neuter. You learn each noun's gender along with the noun.

vir, man: masculinemāter, mother: femininebellum, war: neuter
Why gender matters

A noun's gender decides the endings of any adjective that describes it, because an adjective must match its noun in case, number and gender: vir bonus (a good man), puella bona (a good girl), bellum bonum (a good war). Gender is often a guess from the noun's own ending, but the adjective shows it for certain. A few rules help (A&G §§31–33): names of men, rivers, winds and months are masculine; names of women, cities, countries and trees are usually feminine; and a few 1st-declension nouns for jobs men held, like nauta (sailor) and agricola (farmer), are masculine even though most of their declension is feminine.

Every case has more uses than its basic one. They are on Case Usages. A seventh case, the locative, means "at" and survives only in the names of cities and towns and a few words such as domī, "at home."

What makes cases hard

The same ending can belong to more than one case. Puellae can be genitive singular ("of the girl"), dative singular ("to the girl") or nominative plural ("the girls"). The ending narrows the choices, and the rest of the sentence decides between them: is there already a subject? Is there a verb of giving? The declension pages mark these look-alike forms with a dot so that you can learn to spot them.

Declensions: five families of nouns A&G §§22, 36–37

A declension is a family of nouns that share the same set of endings. Latin has five. To decline a noun is to list its forms in every case, singular and plural. The endings differ from one declension to the next, so before you can read a noun's ending, you need to know which declension it belongs to.

The genitive singular tells you. Every declension has its own genitive ending, which is why the dictionary lists the genitive right after the noun. The genitive also gives you the noun's stem, the part that every other form is built on: take off the genitive ending, and what is left is the stem (A&G §37). This matters most in the 3rd declension, where the nominative often looks different from the rest of the word: rēx, king, has the genitive rēgis, so its stem is rēg-.

Why not the nominative?

The nominative singular is a poor guide to the declension. Equus (2nd), frūctus (4th) and corpus (3rd) all end in -us, but their genitives, equī, frūctūs and corporis, are all different. Always learn a noun with its genitive.

The five declensions share more than they seem to. Some endings work the same way in all of them: the accusative singular ends in -m, and the dative and ablative plural are always the same form. The Five Declensions: Patterns sets all five side by side and lists these shared rules.

Reading a dictionary entry

Dictionaries and vocabulary lists often shorten the genitive to its ending: puella, -ae, f. When the stem changes, as in the 3rd declension, they usually print the whole genitive.
