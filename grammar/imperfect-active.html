The imperfect tense

The Latin imperfect tense describes incomplete action happening in the past. We translate it either like "he was loving" (ongoing past) or "he used to love."

It is formed by adding a -ba- time marker between the incomplete stem and the same personal endings used by the present, with one exception: -m rather than -ō for the 1st singular (amābam). Note that the a in -ba- is a marker of time, and it is the same marker of time seen in the English "was," which can be a useful mnemonic for remembering the imperfect's translation.

In the 3rd conjugation, the linking vowel changes back to -e- from -i- (regēbam), and in the 4th it changes from -ī- to -iē- (audiēbam), though this information is largely unimportant for anything but composition.

What the imperfect means

The imperfect describes a past action that was going on, repeated, or habitual. It shows the action in progress rather than as a single finished event.

The usual translation is "was ___ing": amābat = "she was loving." Depending on the sentence, "used to ___" or "kept ___ing" can fit better: amābat = "she used to love," "she kept loving."

Latin has a separate past tense for single, completed actions ("she loved," once). You'll meet it later; for now, -ba- means an ongoing past.

How to build the imperfect

Take the incomplete stem, add the past time marker -ba-, then a personal ending. The endings are the same as in the present, except that "I" is -m instead of -ō.

Irregular: sum, esse (to be)

sum doesn't use -ba-. Its imperfect is built on er-, with the same endings as every other imperfect: eram, erās, erat, erāmus, erātis, erant. You'll see erat constantly in stories: puella in hortō erat, "the girl was in the garden."
