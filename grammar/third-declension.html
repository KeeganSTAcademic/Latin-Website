What is the third declension?

The third declension is the biggest noun family in Latin, and the least predictable at first glance: its nominative singular can end in almost anything (rēx, corpus, urbs, nōmen, animal, legiō). What stays steady is everything after the nominative.

Always learn the genitive. The genitive singular ends in -is, and dropping it gives you the stem that every other form is built on: rēx, rēgis → rēg-; corpus, corporis → corpor-.

Unlike the first two declensions, gender matters for the endings: masculine and feminine nouns share one pattern, and neuters follow another.

Masculine & feminine

rēx, rēgis, m. king
  • Singular: nom. varies · -is, -ī, -em, -e
  • Plural: -ēs, -um, -ibus, -ēs, -ibus
  • Accusative singular takes -m; the plural nominative and accusative are the same (rēgēs).

Neuter

corpus, corporis, n. body
  • Singular: nom. varies · -is, -ī, acc. = nom., -e
  • Plural: -a, -um, -ibus, -a, -ibus
  • No -m in the accusative: the nominative and accusative are always identical, and the plural ends in -a.

i-stems

Some third-declension nouns are i-stems: their stem once ended in -i, and that i still shows up in a few forms. The most reliable sign is the genitive plural in -ium instead of -um. Allen & Greenough (§§65–72) sort them like this:

  • Pure i-stems, m./f. (§66–67): parisyllabic nouns in -is, meaning the nominative and genitive have the same number of syllables (turris, turris; ignis, ignis). A few keep -im in the accusative and -ī in the ablative.
  • Pure i-stems, neuter (§68–69): nouns in -e, -al, -ar (mare, animal, calcar). They have -ī in the ablative singular, -ium in the genitive plural, and -ia in the nominative and accusative plural.
  • Mixed i-stems (§70–72): declined like rēx in the singular and like i-stems in the plural (-ium, acc. -īs or -ēs). These include monosyllables in -s or -x after a consonant (urbs, ars, pōns, arx), nouns in -ns or -rs (cliēns, cohors), nouns in -tās (cīvitās), and a few others such as nox, dōs, fraus.

For advanced students: how the nominative is formed

The nominative singular looks irregular because sounds changed when endings met the stem. Once you know the rules, most nominatives become predictable from the genitive (A&G §§55–64).

Masculine & feminine: add -s (§56)

Stem ends in c or gc/g + s merge into xdux, ducis (duc-) · rēx, rēgis (rēg-)
Stem ends in t or dthe t/d drops before smīles, mīlitis (mīlit-) · custōs, custōdis (custōd-)
Stem ends in p or bthe consonant stays; s is addedprīnceps, prīncipis (prīncip-)
Two-syllable stemsoften e in the nominative, i in the stemprīnceps / prīncip- · mīles / mīlit-

Neuters (§63–64)

Stems in -in-e for i in the nominativenōmen, nōminis
Most -or- and -er- stemsnominative in -uscorpus, corporis · tempus, temporis · genus, generis · opus, operis

Why corpus but corporis? These stems originally ended in -s (corpos-). Between two vowels, an old Latin s turned into r (rhotacism), so *corposis became corporis, while the nominative kept its s. The same change explains flōs, flōris and mōs, mōris.

Watch out: a nominative in -us doesn't always mean 2nd declension. corpus, corporis is 3rd declension neuter, which is another reason to learn the genitive with every noun.
